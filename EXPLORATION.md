# Notes from the paper

## A removal that acts

The initial question was whether erasing could be an affirmative operation. An ordinary undo quietly removes a decision from the world. I wanted a tool where a removed decision changed the material you were working with.

A ghost outline alone felt insufficient: it would be another visible layer. The absent gesture needed a consequence you could feel while drawing. Turning its direction into a current made that consequence spatial and fairly easy to understand.

The important constraint became: **only absent marks exert a force**. Visible ink remains still. This keeps the instrument from becoming an ordinary fluid simulation, and makes the act of letting go necessary.

## The control canvas

When the first curved threads appeared, it was difficult to know how much came from the hand and how much came from the memory. The counterfactual canvas was a better answer than a numeric intensity indicator. Identical gestures and random seeds are drawn with zero memory force. Switching between the realities shows the intervention directly.

That pairing became a printable artifact: two lives of the same gesture. The saved HTML uses inline SVG and a CSS checkbox to reveal the absent paths. It needs no JavaScript, fonts, downloads or external server.

## Three scores

- **An absent tide**: a loop and a wave, crossed by seven near-horizontal gestures. The output curls and fans outward.
- **The unmade moon**: nested circular memories run in opposite directions. Radial gestures become a wreath with a hollow center.
- **A fault in the paper**: one angular seam bends twelve straight crossings into a disrupted weave.

Measured in the 1000 × 700 coordinate space, the mean endpoint displacement from the control canvas is about 91.5, 84.1 and 97.1 units respectively. Those numbers are only a check that the memories act; they say nothing about how good the image is.

Reversing a current adds another choice without requiring a replacement drawing. Forgetting it brings back blank material locally. Neither operation rewrites ink that is already on the sheet. The instrument has a history rather than a live global filter.

## A practical discovery

The first brush saved every integration point. Some example sheets exceeded normal local-storage limits. Most of the counterfactual threads are straight, so retaining fifty points on each was unnecessary. Simplifying within a 0.18-pixel error budget and rounding stored geometry to two decimals keeps the same endpoints and leaves the visual path within a small fraction of a pixel. The three scores now fit comfortably in browser storage.

An intentionally long zigzag also revealed that brush offsets could place a seed just outside the sheet. Clamping seeds at the boundary makes a study saved from the app valid to reopen.

## Things I chose to leave out

Sound and automatic animation would make a different instrument. The pleasure here is watching a static mark carry the consequence of a removed one. The blank sheet, quiet ink, direction of a gesture, and comparison are enough to explore for a while.

All work after the request to stay local is kept locally. No further pushes or online setup were needed. The current instrument and atlas can be served or opened from the personal folder.

## Cutting an opening

Continuing the exploration suggested a more tactile removal: lifting just the threads beneath a brush. Whole-gesture erasure remains useful, but it makes the shape of an absence a fairly large decision. A small cut lets you sculpt that absence.

The lifting brush clips line segments against a swept capsule, so fast hand movements do not leave holes in the cut. Remaining ink stays exactly where it was. Each removed fragment becomes part of one disconnected memory when the hand lifts. The field samples those paths separately; joining their endpoints would introduce a fictional current through space that was never erased.

The control image needs a corresponding cut. Each removed interval is mapped by its fractional arc length onto the same thread without memory. Comparing the two realities therefore compares equivalent surviving thread intervals, rather than an erased image against an intact one.

**A borrowed opening** plays the new operation as a score. Six graphite gestures form a weave. A diagonal opening is lifted from it. Ten violet gestures cross the opening and bend under the influence of the removed graphite. The gap becomes useful material for a second drawing.

Releasing a partly cut mark remembers only its surviving threads, rather than bringing its already-removed centerline back a second time. Very short fragments also retain both endpoints so they can exert a direction. These small details matter to the rule that only actual absence should act.
